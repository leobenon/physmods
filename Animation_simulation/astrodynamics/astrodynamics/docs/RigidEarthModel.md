# Rigid Earth Rotation Model

## 1. Scope

This model describes the rotational motion of a rigid, axisymmetric Earth
subject to a simplified lunar gravity-gradient torque.

The implementation is based on the MATLAB files:

- `rot2.m`
- `deqrot2.m`

The initial Python implementation aims to reproduce the MATLAB model before
introducing physical or numerical improvements.

---

## 2. State Vector

The propagated state is

$$
\mathbf y =
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_1 &
\omega_2 &
\omega_3 &
\psi &
\varepsilon &
\theta
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
$$

The quantities \(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3\) are the components of the
Earth's angular velocity in the body-fixed principal-axis frame.

The angles \(\psi,\varepsilon,\theta\) describe the Earth's orientation using
the Euler-angle convention of the original MATLAB model.

---

## 3. Time and Angle Constants

$$
1\ \mathrm{day}=86400\ \mathrm{s}
$$

$$
1\ \mathrm{rad}
=
\frac{180}{\pi}\ \mathrm{deg}
=
206264.806\ \mathrm{arcsec}
$$

---

## 4. Earth Parameters

The nominal Earth rotation rate is

$$
\omega_\oplus
=
7.2921151467\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$

The initial obliquity is

$$
\varepsilon_0 = 23.5^\circ.
$$

The inertia-difference ratios are

$$
\gamma_1=\frac{C-B}{A},
\qquad
\gamma_2=\frac{A-C}{B},
\qquad
\gamma_3=\frac{B-A}{C}.
$$

The model uses

$$
\gamma_1=0.003295669,
\qquad
\gamma_2=-0.003295669,
\qquad
\gamma_3=0.
$$

Because \(\gamma_3=0\), the model assumes

$$
A=B,
$$

and therefore treats Earth as an axisymmetric rigid body.

---

## 5. Moon Parameters

The lunar gravitational parameter is approximated by

$$
\mu_M
=
\frac{398.6\times10^{12}}{81.3}
\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}.
$$

The simplified lunar orbit uses

$$
r_M=3.8\times10^8\ \mathrm{m},
$$

$$
n_M=2.661707223\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},
$$

and an orbital inclination

$$
i_M=28^\circ.
$$

The Moon is initially modeled as moving on a circular orbit.

## 6. Elementary Rotation Matrices

The implementation uses active, right-handed rotations acting on column
vectors:

$$
\mathbf v' = \mathbf R\,\mathbf v.
$$

Rotation about the first axis:

$$
\mathbf R_x(\alpha)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\
0 & \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Rotation about the second axis:

$$
\mathbf R_y(\beta)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\beta & 0 & \sin\beta \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
-\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Rotation about the third axis:

$$
\mathbf R_z(\gamma)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma & 0 \\
\sin\gamma & \cos\gamma & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Every proper rotation matrix satisfies

$$
\mathbf R^\mathsf{T}\mathbf R=\mathbf I,
\qquad
\det(\mathbf R)=+1,
\qquad
\mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^\mathsf{T}.
$$

### Matrices in the original MATLAB model

The lunar-orbit inclination matrix is

$$
\mathbf A=\mathbf R_x(i_M).
$$

The sidereal-angle matrix is

$$
\mathbf B=\mathbf R_z(-\theta).
$$

The lunar position is transformed according to

$$
\mathbf r_M^{\F}
=
\mathbf R_z(-\theta)
\mathbf R_x(i_M)
\mathbf r_M^{\I}.
$$

The superscripts \(I\) and \(F\) denote inertial and Earth-fixed
coordinates, respectively.

## 7. Simplified Lunar Orbit

The Moon is modeled as moving on a circular orbit with constant radius

$$
r_M = 3.8\times10^8\ \mathrm{m}
$$

and constant orbital angular rate

$$
n_M = 2.661707223\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$

Its inertial position is

$$
\mathbf r_M^I(t)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_M\cos(n_M t) \\
r_M\sin(n_M t) \\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The corresponding period is

$$
T_M = \frac{2\pi}{n_M}.
$$

The inclination is not included directly in this position model. It is
introduced later through the transformation

$$
\mathbf r_M^F
=
\mathbf R_z(-\theta)
\mathbf R_x(i_M)
\mathbf r_M^I.
$$

This keeps the lunar orbit model independent of the Earth's orientation.

## 8. Inertial-to-Body Frame Transformation

Let

- \(I\) denote the inertial reference frame,
- \(B\) denote the Earth body-fixed frame.

The Moon position generated by the circular orbit model is initially
expressed in the inertial frame:

$$
\mathbf r_M^I(t)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_M\cos(n_M t) \\
r_M\sin(n_M t) \\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The direction cosine matrix that transforms inertial coordinates into
body-fixed coordinates is

$$
\mathbf C_{B\leftarrow I}
=
\mathbf R_z(-\theta)\mathbf R_x(i_M).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbf r_M^B
=
\mathbf C_{B\leftarrow I}\mathbf r_M^I.
$$

Explicitly,

$$
\mathbf r_M^B
=
\mathbf R_z(-\theta)
\mathbf R_x(i_M)
\mathbf r_M^I.
$$

The reverse transformation is

$$
\mathbf r_M^I
=
\mathbf C_{B\leftarrow I}^{\mathsf T}
\mathbf r_M^B,
$$

because every proper direction cosine matrix satisfies

$$
\mathbf C^{-1}=\mathbf C^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Rotations preserve vector lengths, so

$$
\left\|\mathbf r_M^B\right\|
=
\left\|\mathbf r_M^I\right\|
=
r_M.
$$

## 9. Lunar Gravity-Gradient Torque

The Moon exerts a gravity-gradient torque on the nonspherical Earth.

Let the Earth's inertia tensor in its principal-axis frame be

$$
\mathbf I =
\begin{bmatrix}
A & 0 & 0 \\
0 & B & 0 \\
0 & 0 & C
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Let the Moon position expressed in the body-fixed frame be

$$
\mathbf r_M^B =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_M \\
y_M \\
z_M
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
r_M = \left\|\mathbf r_M^B\right\|.
$$

The physical gravity-gradient torque is

$$
\mathbf M_M^B
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
\left(
\mathbf r_M^B
\times
\mathbf I\mathbf r_M^B
\right).
$$

For a diagonal inertia tensor, its components are

$$
M_1
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
(C-B)y_Mz_M,
$$

$$
M_2
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
(A-C)z_Mx_M,
$$

$$
M_3
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
(B-A)x_My_M.
$$

The MATLAB model does not use the physical torque directly. Instead, each
component is divided by the corresponding principal moment:

$$
d_1 = \frac{M_1}{A},
\qquad
d_2 = \frac{M_2}{B},
\qquad
d_3 = \frac{M_3}{C}.
$$

Using

$$
\gamma_1=\frac{C-B}{A},
\qquad
\gamma_2=\frac{A-C}{B},
\qquad
\gamma_3=\frac{B-A}{C},
$$

the normalized torque terms become

$$
d_1
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
\gamma_1 y_Mz_M,
$$

$$
d_2
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
\gamma_2 z_Mx_M,
$$

$$
d_3
=
\frac{3\mu_M}{r_M^5}
\gamma_3 x_My_M.
$$

These terms have units of angular acceleration and enter Euler's rotational
equations directly.

Because the rigid-Earth model assumes \(A=B\),

$$
\gamma_3=0,
$$

and therefore

$$
d_3=0.
$$

## 10. Euler's Rotational Equations

For a rigid body expressed in its principal-axis frame,

$$
\mathbf I =
\begin{bmatrix}
A & 0 & 0 \\
0 & B & 0 \\
0 & 0 & C
\end{bmatrix},
$$

Euler's rotational equations are

$$
A\dot{\omega}_1 + (C-B)\omega_2\omega_3 = M_1,
$$

$$
B\dot{\omega}_2 + (A-C)\omega_3\omega_1 = M_2,
$$

$$
C\dot{\omega}_3 + (B-A)\omega_1\omega_2 = M_3.
$$

Dividing each equation by the corresponding principal moment gives

$$
\dot{\omega}_1
=
-\gamma_1\omega_2\omega_3+d_1,
$$

$$
\dot{\omega}_2
=
-\gamma_2\omega_3\omega_1+d_2,
$$

$$
\dot{\omega}_3
=
-\gamma_3\omega_1\omega_2+d_3,
$$

where

$$
d_1=\frac{M_1}{A},
\qquad
d_2=\frac{M_2}{B},
\qquad
d_3=\frac{M_3}{C}.
$$

The nonlinear products such as

$$
\omega_2\omega_3
$$

represent inertial coupling between rotations about different principal axes.

For an axisymmetric Earth,

$$
A=B,
$$

so

$$
\gamma_3=0.
$$

Without a third-axis torque,

$$
\dot{\omega}_3=0,
$$

meaning the dominant spin component remains constant in this rigid,
axisymmetric model.

## 11. Euler-Angle Kinematics

The rotational dynamics determine the body-frame angular-velocity
components

$$
\omega_1,\quad \omega_2,\quad \omega_3.
$$

The Earth's orientation is described by the Euler angles

$$
\psi,\quad \varepsilon,\quad \theta.
$$

The original MATLAB model uses

$$
\dot{\psi}
=
-
\frac{
\omega_1\sin\theta+\omega_2\cos\theta
}{
\sin\varepsilon
},
$$

$$
\dot{\varepsilon}
=
-
\left(
\omega_1\cos\theta-\omega_2\sin\theta
\right),
$$

and

$$
\dot{\theta}
=
\frac{
\omega_1\sin\theta+\omega_2\cos\theta
}{
\tan\varepsilon
}
+
\omega_\oplus.
$$

The first two equations describe changes in precession and obliquity
caused by the transverse angular-velocity components.

The third equation contains the nominal Earth rotation rate

$$
\omega_\oplus,
$$

together with a smaller correction caused by the transverse motion.

These Euler-angle equations are singular when

$$
\sin\varepsilon=0.
$$

This is a coordinate singularity of the Euler-angle representation, not
a physical singularity of the Earth.

## 12. Numerical Integration

The complete state is

$$
\mathbf y =
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_1 &
\omega_2 &
\omega_3 &
\psi &
\varepsilon &
\theta
\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The governing initial-value problem is

$$
\dot{\mathbf y}
=
\mathbf f(t,\mathbf y),
$$

with initial state

$$
\mathbf y(0)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
10^{-6}\omega_\oplus \\
0 \\
\omega_\oplus \\
0 \\
23.5^\circ \\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The original MATLAB script integrates for

$$
70\ \mathrm{days}
$$

and limits the maximum integration step to

$$
3600\ \mathrm{s}.
$$

The Python implementation initially uses SciPy's adaptive DOP853
integrator.

The solver controls the local numerical error according to a scale of
the form

$$
\mathrm{atol}
+
\mathrm{rtol}\left|y_i\right|.
$$

The maximum step limits the solver's internal timestep, while the output
step only determines the times at which the solution is returned for
analysis and plotting.

The initial Python tolerances are

$$
\mathrm{rtol}=10^{-10},
\qquad
\mathrm{atol}=10^{-13}.
$$

These values are stricter than the default tolerances and are used to
obtain a reliable reference trajectory before comparing other
integration methods.
